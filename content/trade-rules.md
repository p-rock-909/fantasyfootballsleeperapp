# Fantasy Football Trade Evaluation Rules

## Purpose

Use this framework to evaluate whether a fantasy-football trade creates fair and useful value for **both teams**, rather than judging it only by player names, last week’s points, or a generic trade calculator.

A good trade can be uneven in raw player value while still being rational for both teams if it improves each manager’s starting lineup, addresses different positional needs, changes risk appropriately, or fits different competitive timelines. The goal is to identify:

- Whether each side receives reasonable value in the league’s format.
- Which team improves its likely weekly and rest-of-season lineup.
- Whether the trade is fair, slightly tilted, materially lopsided, or potentially collusive.
- What adjustments could make the proposal more balanced and more likely to be accepted.

> Core principle: evaluate the difference between the lineups each manager can start **before and after** the trade—not merely the sum of player rankings.

---

## Required Inputs

Gather these inputs before making a recommendation. If a detail is unknown, state the assumption instead of treating it as fact.

### League context

- League type: redraft, keeper, dynasty, best ball, or guillotine.
- Number of teams and roster sizes.
- Scoring: PPR, half-PPR, standard, points per first down, TE premium, bonuses, return-yard scoring, or other deviations.
- Starting lineup requirements: QB, RB, WR, TE, FLEX, superflex, D/ST, K, IDP, and bench spots.
- Trade deadline, playoff format, playoff weeks, and whether there are consolation/rebuild incentives.
- Waiver-wire strength: shallow leagues make replacement options more available; deep leagues make usable starters more valuable.

### Team context for both managers

- Full roster, including injured reserve and key bench players.
- Current record, standings, points scored, and playoff odds if available.
- Remaining bye weeks and immediate lineup gaps.
- Current weekly starters and realistic replacement-level options.
- Position surpluses and weaknesses.
- Competitive objective: immediate win, playoff push, title contention, retool, or rebuild.
- Remaining FAAB, waiver priority, and ability to replace depth after the deal.

### Player context for every asset

- Position, team, role, expected snap/route/touch share, and depth-chart competition.
- Rest-of-season projection and a range of plausible outcomes—not a single number only.
- Current injury, practice status, recovery timeline, suspension risk, and durability concerns.
- Quarterback quality, offensive-line health, team scoring environment, and coaching tendencies.
- Upcoming schedule, bye week, playoff schedule, and role-specific matchups.
- Age, contract/security, draft-pick value, and long-term trajectory in keeper/dynasty formats.
- Draft-pick details in dynasty: year, round, projected range, format, and whether the pick is a manager’s own or another team’s.

---

## Evaluation Sequence

Evaluate a trade in this order:

1. **Confirm format and timeline.** A redraft trade cannot be judged by dynasty values, and a contender should not use the same lens as a rebuilding dynasty team.
2. **Project each player’s role.** Start with sustainable usage and health, not recent fantasy points alone.
3. **Estimate rest-of-season value.** Use scoring-format-specific projections, positional replacement level, consistency, upside, and downside.
4. **Build each team’s before-and-after lineup.** Identify exactly who starts and who moves to the bench after the trade.
5. **Measure marginal starting-lineup change.** A trade is valuable when it upgrades active starters more than it weakens them elsewhere.
6. **Evaluate depth, injury protection, and byes.** A paper win can be a practical loss if the trade leaves a team unable to survive an injury or bye.
7. **Apply schedule, risk, and timing.** Short-term urgency and playoff schedules may matter more than broad rest-of-season rankings.
8. **Assess fairness and propose adjustments.** State who benefits, why, and what asset(s) would narrow a real gap.

## Core Rules

### 1. Evaluate starter value, not aggregate bench value

A 2-for-1 trade should not be accepted as equal just because two smaller player values add up to one star’s value.

- Identify the player each team must start because of the deal.
- Count the upgrade from the outgoing starter to the incoming starter.
- Discount excess depth that remains on the bench and cannot be started.
- Give real value to depth only when it covers multiple starters, upcoming byes, injury exposure, or flex usage.
- In shallow leagues, elite consolidation is usually more valuable because replacement-level players are accessible.
- In deep leagues, usable depth and reliable starters carry more value because replacements are scarce.

**Illustration:** Trading two WR2/WR3-type players for an elite WR can be good for the team with receiver depth if only one of the outgoing players was regularly starting. It can be good for the other team if it upgrades two starting slots and they lack playable depth. It is not automatically fair simply because a trade chart says the totals match.

### 2. Opportunity and role outweigh recent box scores

Assess whether a player’s production is repeatable.

Prioritize:

- Snap share, route participation, targets, carries, touch share, and red-zone/goal-line role.
- Whether usage is rising, stable, or declining over multiple games.
- Injury-driven openings, depth-chart changes, quarterback changes, trades, coaching decisions, or teammate returns.
- Whether a recent outburst came from sustainable volume versus a long touchdown, turnover, broken play, or unusual game script.

Downgrade a player whose market value relies on unsustainable touchdown efficiency, low-volume big plays, a temporary absence, or role uncertainty.

### 3. Format changes value materially

Apply the league’s actual scoring rules before declaring a winner.

| Format factor | Players generally upgraded | Players generally downgraded |
|---|---|---|
| Full PPR | Target earners, receiving RBs, high-volume slot WRs, route-heavy TEs | Early-down-only RBs with little receiving work |
| Half PPR | Balanced volume and touchdown profiles | Extreme specialists at either end |
| Standard | Touchdown/yardage upside, early-down and goal-line RBs | Low-depth-of-target volume WRs and pass-catching-only RBs |
| Superflex / 2QB | Starting QBs, especially durable multi-year starters | Non-elite single-QB assets relative to scarce QB starters |
| TE premium | Target-earning and elite TEs | Replacement-level TEs with weak routes |
| Deep benches | Contingency backs, stashes, future picks | Easily replaceable streamers |
| Shallow benches | Immediate starters and weekly ceiling | Long-term stashes without a startable role |

Do not import a player valuation from a different format without adjusting it.

### 4. Positional scarcity is local to the league

Scarcity is not simply “RB is scarce” or “TE is scarce.” Calculate it from the actual league.

- Compare a player with the best freely available replacement at the same position.
- Account for lineup requirements: two RB plus two WR plus flex creates different scarcity than three WR plus two flex.
- In superflex, starting quarterbacks can have a much larger marginal value than their raw projected points suggest.
- In TE premium, an elite target-earning TE may create a larger lineup advantage than a similarly ranked WR.
- Consider whether a manager can stream the position effectively after trading away a starter.

Use **value over replacement** conceptually:

\[
\text{Marginal Player Value} \approx \text{Projected Fantasy Points} - \text{Best Realistic Replacement Points}
\]

Use the available replacement for that league and roster, not an abstract replacement player.

### 5. Compare ranges of outcomes, not only median projections

Every player has a realistic floor, median, and ceiling.

- High-floor assets: stable roles, reliable volume, healthy status, secure quarterback/offense, and less touchdown dependence.
- High-ceiling assets: explosive role, deep targets, rushing quarterback profile, goal-line work, or an emerging opportunity.
- High-risk assets: injuries, uncertain usage, committee roles, suspensions, quarterback instability, or fragile depth-chart position.

A manager projected to lose this week may rationally prefer ceiling. A first-place team with a strong roster may rationally prefer floor and durability. A trade can be sensible if it deliberately exchanges safety for upside or vice versa.

### 6. Health and news can change a deal immediately

Before approving a trade, verify current circumstances.

- Use official injury designations, practice reports, transaction news, credible beat reports, and game-day inactive lists.
- Identify whether an injured player is expected to return at full capacity, is at risk of a snap count, or faces reinjury/recurrence concerns.
- Separate temporary injury replacement value from durable role value.
- Consider injuries to teammates: QB, offensive line, target competition, and backfield competition can materially affect a player’s outlook.
- State uncertainty: **confirmed**, **likely**, **questionable**, or **speculative**.

Do not accept a player’s pre-injury ranking as their current value without a credible recovery and usage projection.

### 7. Schedule and game environment are contextual modifiers

Use schedule to break close decisions, not to override major role differences.

- Consider upcoming opponent strength against the player’s role, expected scoring environment, game spreads, pace, weather, travel, and team implied totals.
- For a contender, emphasize the weeks needed to reach or win the playoffs.
- For a team fighting for a playoff spot, immediate 2–4 week production can outweigh a later favorable playoff schedule.
- For a player with a stable role, a difficult schedule is usually a modest downgrade—not an automatic sell.
- Recheck defensive trends, injuries, and opponent quality; season-long “points allowed” can be misleading.

### 8. Trade for a clear objective

A trade should have a named purpose. Common valid objectives:

- Upgrade an active starter at a weak position.
- Consolidate surplus depth into an elite weekly lineup advantage.
- Turn an elite asset into multiple legitimate starters when depth is the real issue.
- Cover an injury or multiple bye weeks without surrendering future value unnecessarily.
- Move volatile players for stable production when protecting a strong standing.
- Buy upside when the manager needs variance to catch the leaders.
- In dynasty, shift from aging production to youth/picks in a rebuild, or from future assets to points in a title window.

Reject or renegotiate a trade that does not solve a roster problem unless the incoming value is clearly too good to decline.

## Redraft Rules

### Redraft valuation priorities

In most redraft leagues, prioritize:

1. Expected points during the remaining regular season and playoffs.
2. Starting-lineup improvement.
3. Role security, health, and weekly floor.
4. Ceiling needed for the manager’s matchup and roster situation.
5. Byes, schedule, and ability to replace lost depth.

Age, contracts, and future draft picks usually have no value unless the league has keeper rules.

### Redraft trade tests

A proposal is generally favorable when it:

- Clearly improves at least one starting position without creating an unfillable new weakness.
- Leaves enough RB/WR depth to absorb reasonable injury or bye risk.
- Replaces a volatile or declining role with a more secure weekly role.
- Converts bench surplus into points that can actually enter the lineup.

A proposal needs caution when it:

- Requires starting a waiver-level player at another position.
- Leaves a roster with only one viable RB or WR during an injury-heavy period.
- Sells a healthy volume player for a player recovering from injury without enough compensation.
- Pays full market price for a player whose recent output is mostly touchdown-driven.

## Dynasty and Keeper Rules

### Dynasty valuation priorities

In dynasty, evaluate both present and future value. Include:

- Age and position-specific aging curve.
- Long-term role security, contract situation, quarterback situation, and competition.
- Talent profile and likelihood of future target/touch volume.
- Draft-pick year, round, projected range, and uncertainty.
- Team timeline: contending, middle-of-pack, retooling, or rebuilding.
- Superflex and TE-premium effects on long-term scarcity.

### Timeline alignment

| Team status | Usually prioritize | Usually de-emphasize |
|---|---|---|
| True contender | Reliable current production, playoff-week contributors, healthy veterans, lineup floor | Distant draft picks and developmental assets with little current role |
| Fringe contender | Players who can help now without destroying future flexibility, age-appropriate upgrades | All-in overpays for short-term rentals |
| Rebuild | Young players, insulated value, early draft picks, multi-year upside | Aging/declining veterans with short windows |
| Productive struggle / retool | Liquidity, picks, young players with value growth potential | Expensive aging players who do not materially change the title odds |

Do not call a trade unfair merely because a contender trades future value for current points or a rebuilder does the reverse. Assess whether the compensation makes sense for both timelines.

### Dynasty pick rules

- Value picks as ranges, not fixed numbers; a future first could become early, middle, or late.
- Discount picks farther into the future for uncertainty, but do not treat them as worthless.
- Upgrade a pick when the sending roster is weak, aging, or lacks future assets; downgrade it when the sending roster is dominant and deep.
- Specify whether the pick is projected early/mid/late, the basis for that view, and the uncertainty.
- In superflex, premium young QBs and early rookie picks generally carry additional strategic value because QB starters are scarce.

## Fairness and Collusion Screen

A fair trade does not require exact mathematical equality. It should have a plausible good-faith rationale for both managers.

### Usually fair or acceptable

- Both teams address distinct, visible roster needs.
- One side gets a star; the other gets multiple players who enter its starting lineup.
- A contender receives current production while a rebuilder receives youth/picks.
- The valuation gap is modest and explainable through format, timing, risk tolerance, or roster construction.
- Both managers retain normal competitive incentives.

### Needs review or adjustment

- One side gives an elite healthy starter for bench-level pieces that will not start.
- The trade is based on obsolete information about an injury, suspension, or depth-chart change.
- The deal creates an extreme one-sided projected lineup change with no clear future compensation.
- A manager appears to be dumping productive assets to an opponent without a reasonable return.

### Potential collusion indicators

- The trade lacks a credible fantasy rationale and gives overwhelming value to a direct competitor.
- Managers coordinate roster moves to manipulate standings, playoff opponents, waivers, or future matchups.
- A manager gives away valuable assets while abandoning competition.
- There is evidence of compensation outside league rules.

Do not accuse managers of collusion based solely on a trade you would not make. Flag the issue only when the imbalance is extreme and there is no plausible good-faith explanation.

## Trade Scoring Framework

Use this as a consistent starting point. Score the impact **for each team separately**; do not use it as a substitute for judgment.

| Category | Points | Evaluation question |
|---|---:|---|
| Starting-lineup impact | −30 to +30 | How much does the trade change this team’s projected active lineup? |
| Rest-of-season player value | −20 to +20 | Is the incoming package stronger after format adjustments? |
| Positional scarcity / replacement value | −15 to +15 | Does the team gain a hard-to-replace starter or lose one? |
| Health and role certainty | −10 to +10 | Does the trade improve durability and secure opportunity? |
| Depth and bye-week resilience | −10 to +10 | Can the roster withstand normal injuries/byes afterward? |
| Schedule and timing | −5 to +5 | Does it help in the weeks that matter for this team? |
| Strategic fit | −10 to +10 | Does it fit title odds, risk needs, and dynasty timeline? |

### Interpretation

- **+20 or more:** Strongly beneficial for that team.
- **+8 to +19:** Favorable and rational.
- **−7 to +7:** Roughly neutral; preference and risk tolerance likely decide it.
- **−8 to −19:** Unfavorable unless the team has a specific non-obvious strategic reason.
- **−20 or less:** Materially harmful; seek a major adjustment or decline.

A trade can score positively for both teams if it addresses different constraints. A trade that scores heavily positive for one side and heavily negative for the other needs a clear compensating explanation or revision.

## Package-Trade Rules

### When trading up for a star

Trading multiple players for one elite player is usually justified when:

- The acquiring team has genuine starter-level depth, and at least one outgoing player would be benched after the trade.
- The star produces a meaningful positional or weekly ceiling advantage.
- The acquiring team still has enough usable depth to cover injuries and byes.
- The opponent gains two or more players who improve its starting lineup.

Avoid it when:

- Both outgoing players are must-start assets and the remaining replacement is weak.
- The elite player has unresolved injury or role risk.
- The trade leaves the acquiring roster with an unmanageable position shortage.

### When trading down for depth

Trading a star for several players is usually justified when:

- At least two incoming players become reliable starters.
- The team has a genuine depth crisis due to injuries, byes, or roster imbalance.
- The incoming players have stable roles and are not simply several low-upside bench pieces.
- The team can replace lost elite production with aggregate improvements across multiple slots.

Avoid it when:

- The return is three or more players competing for one starting spot.
- The package contains several touchdown-dependent or role-unstable players.
- The star’s manager is selling at a temporary low point without needing depth.

### Include a throw-in only when it matters

A “throw-in” should materially improve fairness, lineup utility, future value, or negotiation comfort. Do not add assets just to make player counts equal.

Useful throw-ins include:

- A playable depth RB/WR who covers a concrete bye/injury need.
- A future pick in keeper/dynasty formats.
- A handcuff tied to an incoming high-value RB.
- A position upgrade that solves a specific scarcity issue.

## Negotiation Rules

### Build offers from the other manager’s perspective

Before proposing a trade:

- Identify their weak starting positions, injury problems, bye weeks, and surplus positions.
- Understand whether they are contending or rebuilding.
- Offer assets that solve a real need instead of sending random bench pieces.
- Do not assume another manager values your surplus players as highly as you do.
- Use market evidence and recent role changes to set a realistic opening offer.

### Make clear, good-faith proposals

- Lead with a reasonable offer rather than an obviously lopsided “fishing” offer.
- Explain the mutual benefit briefly: for example, “You gain a starting RB for your bye week, while I consolidate WR depth.”
- Ask whether the manager prefers a different player or position if the first offer is declined.
- Avoid emotional arguments based on a single breakout or slump.
- Reassess the offer immediately after new injury news, depth-chart changes, or major quarterback changes.

### Negotiate in ranges

If a trade is close but uneven, propose one of these changes:

- Add a small depth player to the side that is light.
- Swap an unstable player for a more secure player in the same tier.
- Add or remove a draft pick in dynasty/keeper formats.
- Change the main asset from a high-risk player to a steadier comparable option.
- Make the deal conditional on confirmed injury news only if league rules permit it.

## Recommended Output Format

Use this structure for every trade evaluation.

## Trade summary

- **Team A sends:** [assets]
- **Team B sends:** [assets]
- **League assumptions:** [format, scoring, teams, starters, redraft/dynasty]
- **Data/news cutoff:** [date and time]
- **Verdict:** Accept / Accept if adjusted / Close but preference-based / Decline / Needs commissioner review

## Team-by-team impact

| Team | Before trade | After trade | Main lineup change | Depth change | Strategic effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Team A | Key starters and weakness | Key starters and weakness | Specific upgrade/downgrade | Stronger/weaker/unchanged | Fit with record and timeline |
| Team B | Key starters and weakness | Key starters and weakness | Specific upgrade/downgrade | Stronger/weaker/unchanged | Fit with record and timeline |

## Asset analysis

| Asset | Role and usage | ROS outlook | Floor / ceiling | Main risk | Format/timeline adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Player or pick | Usage and team context | Short analysis | High/medium/low | Injury, role, schedule, age | Relevant PPR/SF/dynasty note |

## Verdict rationale

For each team, explain:

- The measurable lineup impact.
- Why the incoming assets fit or do not fit its needs.
- The health, role, and schedule assumptions.
- Which side has the edge, if any, and how large it is.

## Adjustment options

Provide 1–3 practical ways to balance the deal, such as:

- Add [specific type of player/pick] to Team B’s side.
- Substitute [riskier player] for a similarly valued but more stable player.
- Change a future pick from a later to earlier round.
- Remove a non-starting throw-in that unnecessarily increases the gap.

## Contingencies

List clear if/then changes:

- If [injured player] is ruled out for multiple weeks, upgrade [replacement] and revisit the deal.
- If [player] practices fully and is cleared without a snap limit, reduce injury discount.
- If the trade occurs after the key bye week or deadline, update short-term lineup value.
- If a future pick’s projected range changes materially, revalue the dynasty side.

## Quality-Control Checklist

Before issuing a final trade recommendation, verify:

- [ ] The league format, scoring, roster requirements, and timeline were identified.
- [ ] The evaluation used rest-of-season or relevant-window value, not season-long totals alone.
- [ ] Player roles are based on current usage, depth chart, and reliable news.
- [ ] Current injuries, practice statuses, suspensions, and teammate absences were considered.
- [ ] Each team’s actual before-and-after starters were identified.
- [ ] Bench assets were discounted if they do not change a starting lineup.
- [ ] Positional scarcity was evaluated using realistic waiver/replacement options.
- [ ] The team’s record, urgency, risk tolerance, and dynasty timeline were considered.
- [ ] The verdict distinguishes a subjective close trade from a materially lopsided one.
- [ ] Proposed adjustments are concrete and preserve a plausible benefit for both managers.
- [ ] Any uncertain assumption is labeled rather than presented as fact.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Treating trade-calculator totals as a final verdict instead of a starting reference.
- Comparing only player names or recent fantasy points.
- Ignoring who actually replaces each outgoing player in the lineup.
- Counting several bench players as equal to one elite starter without lineup utility.
- Applying PPR, superflex, or dynasty values to the wrong league format.
- Ignoring injuries, snap limits, teammate returns, and depth-chart uncertainty.
- Overweighting a difficult or easy matchup while underweighting role security.
- Selling a strong player immediately after a low-scoring outlier when usage is unchanged.
- Buying a player at peak market value after a touchdown-driven outlier.
- Calling an uneven but strategically rational contender/rebuilder trade “collusion.”
- Approving an extremely lopsided deal without asking whether there is a credible fantasy rationale.

## Data Source Guidance

Use current, credible sources in this order:

1. Official NFL/team injury reports, transactions, practice participation, and game-day inactive lists.
2. Direct coach/team statements and reliable local beat reporting for role and depth-chart context.
3. Snap counts, route participation, target/carry shares, red-zone usage, and play-by-play data.
4. Format-specific rest-of-season rankings, trade value charts, and projections from multiple reputable analysts.
5. Betting markets, projected totals, spreads, and schedule tools as contextual inputs rather than certainty.
6. The league’s actual rosters, standings, waiver pool, lineup rules, and deadline.

When sources disagree, use the most recent and directly verifiable information. Explain the uncertainty and show how it changes the trade conclusion.

---

## Short Decision Rule

Approve or pursue a trade when all three statements are true:

1. It improves your likely active lineup or meaningfully advances your league-specific strategy.
2. The value you surrender is reasonable after accounting for role, health, format, depth, and timing.
3. You can explain why the other manager might rationally accept it.

If any of these statements is not true, decline the trade, wait for better information, or revise the package.
