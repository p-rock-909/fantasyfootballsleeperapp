# Fantasy Football Waiver-Wire Recommendation Rules

## Purpose

Use these rules to rank available players for a fantasy-football waiver claim or short-term pickup, with an emphasis on **value for the next matchup** while preserving upside for the rest of the season. Treat recent box-score production as evidence, not the conclusion: prioritize opportunity, role stability, health, and matchup context.

> Output should distinguish between a one-week starter, a multi-week injury replacement, a speculative stash, and a player who should be avoided despite a recent big game.

---

## Required Inputs

For every available player, gather as much of the following as possible:

- League settings: roster size, scoring (PPR / half-PPR / standard), starting requirements, bench size, FAAB or waiver-priority system, and any special rules (superflex, TE premium, bonuses).
- Player information: position, NFL team, opponent, projected role, availability/ownership, and upcoming bye week.
- Recent usage: offensive snap share, routes run / route participation, targets, carries, touches, goal-line work, red-zone touches, third-down role, and two-minute-drill role.
- Usage trend: the same indicators over the last 2–4 games, not only last week.
- Team context: depth chart, injuries, returns from injury, trades, coaching comments, quarterback status, offensive line health, and team scoring environment.
- Matchup context: opponent strength against the relevant position and role, expected game script, implied team total, pace, spread, weather, and travel when meaningful.
- Health/news: official injury report, practice participation, credible beat reporting, inactives, suspension status, and coach statements.
- Roster context: the fantasy manager’s starting needs, injury/bye-week problems, bench flexibility, and whether the player being dropped has meaningful future value.

If a data point is unavailable, label it as unknown rather than inventing certainty.

---

## Evaluation Order

Evaluate candidates in this order. Earlier items should generally outweigh later items.

1. **Role and opportunity** — Can the player realistically receive enough snaps, routes, targets, carries, or touchdown opportunities to matter?
2. **Change in role** — Did an injury, depth-chart move, trade, suspension, or coaching decision create a larger role that is likely to continue?
3. **Health and availability** — Is the player likely to play, and is the expanded role actually available this week?
4. **Next-week matchup and game environment** — Does the opponent and expected game script support the player’s specific role?
5. **Talent and efficiency** — Does the player appear capable of converting opportunity into fantasy production?
6. **Rest-of-season upside** — If the player hits, can they retain a useful role beyond one game?
7. **Acquisition cost and roster fit** — Is the player worth the waiver priority, FAAB, or roster spot relative to alternatives?

Do not rank a player highly solely because of one touchdown, one long reception, one broken play, or a misleading final stat line.

## Core Rules

### 1. Opportunity beats last week’s fantasy points

Prioritize sustainable workload over recent points.

- For running backs, favor snap share, total touches, carries near the goal line, passing-down work, and whether the player handled late-game snaps.
- For wide receivers and tight ends, favor route participation, routes per dropback, target share, air-yard share, alignment, and red-zone targets.
- For quarterbacks, favor expected dropbacks, rushing attempts, designed runs, goal-line usage, offensive pace, and likelihood of a competitive/high-scoring game.
- For D/ST, favor pressure/sack potential, turnover-prone opposing quarterbacks, low opponent implied total, and game-script support.
- For kickers, favor implied team total, indoor/good-weather conditions, an offense able to cross midfield but not always finish drives, and job security.

A player with modest points but a clear rise in snaps/routes/touches is often a better add than a player who scored on limited usage.

### 2. Identify real role changes early

Raise a player’s priority when there is evidence of an actionable role change:

- A starter is ruled out or likely to miss time, and the candidate is clearly first in line.
- The candidate replaced an injured player during the game and stayed in for meaningful snaps.
- The candidate’s snap share, route participation, or touch share rose across multiple weeks.
- A veteran lost work because of performance, a coaching decision, or a depth-chart change.
- A trade, release, suspension, or return-from-injury timeline alters the pecking order.
- The player moved into two-minute, third-down, goal-line, or red-zone duties.

Downgrade apparent role changes if they resulted only from a blowout, garbage time, a temporary injury, an unusual game plan, or one missing teammate who is expected back immediately.

### 3. Verify injury news before recommending

Injury information can create both the best claims and the biggest traps.

- Prefer official team/NFL injury designations, practice participation, and confirmed inactive reports over rumor.
- Treat "did not practice" differently depending on whether it was a rest day, a new injury, or a recurring issue.
- Upgrade backups when the starter is ruled out, placed on IR, suspended, or repeatedly unable to practice.
- Be more cautious when the starter is merely limited or expected to play; committees often remain unpredictable.
- Downgrade a candidate who is limited in practice, returning from a soft-tissue injury, or at risk of a snap count.
- Note when a player’s own quarterback, offensive line, or key teammate is injured, because that can reduce the value of the whole offense.

State confidence explicitly: **confirmed**, **probable**, **uncertain**, or **speculative**.

### 4. Use matchup as a tiebreaker, not the foundation

A strong matchup helps only if the player has a viable role.

- First establish projected opportunity; then use matchup to separate similar candidates.
- Favor running backs against defenses that allow efficient rushing, receiving production to backs, or frequent red-zone opportunities.
- Favor receivers/tight ends against defenses vulnerable to the player’s alignment or role (for example, slot coverage, outside boundary coverage, linebacker/safety coverage, or TE targets).
- Favor passing-game players in games with high projected totals, close spreads, fast pace, and likely pass-heavy scripts.
- Favor early-down backs in games where their team is favored and likely to protect a lead.
- Favor receiving backs and volume receivers when their team is an underdog and likely to throw more.
- Downgrade offenses facing elite pass rushes, likely low-scoring environments, severe weather, or unstable quarterback play.

Avoid relying only on season-long “fantasy points allowed.” Check recent defensive health, opponent quality, pace, and whether a defense’s apparent weakness is actually volume-driven.

### 5. Separate floor, ceiling, and role security

Every recommendation should include a fantasy profile:

| Profile | Typical signals | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| High floor | Stable snaps/routes/touches, short-area targets, established role | Need a safe weekly fill-in |
| High ceiling | Deep targets, rushing upside, goal-line work, explosive offense | Need upside or are projected to lose |
| One-week stream | Favorable matchup but limited long-term role | Bye-week or injury replacement |
| Multi-week replacement | Clear temporary starting role after injury | Aggressive waiver target |
| Speculative stash | Talented player with unclear current workload but a path to role growth | Deep bench or forward-looking roster |
| Avoid / trap | Touchdown-dependent production, falling usage, unclear health, no durable path | Do not spend meaningful resources |

Do not call a player “safe” if their projection depends on a touchdown, an unresolved injury, or a role that has not been confirmed.

## Position-Specific Rules

### Running backs

Prioritize, in order:

1. Clear lead-back role after an injury or depth-chart change.
2. Combined rushing and receiving workload.
3. Goal-line and red-zone role.
4. Passing-down and two-minute role in PPR formats.
5. Team scoring environment and expected game script.
6. Offensive-line health and rushing efficiency.

Important indicators:

- Snap share and touch share.
- Carries inside the 10 and inside the 5.
- Targets, routes, and long-down-and-distance usage.
- Whether the RB played in neutral, trailing, and leading scripts.
- Whether work is split among multiple backs.

Warnings:

- A backup may be a poor claim if the team uses a three-way committee.
- A goal-line touchdown on five touches is not proof of a reliable role.
- A two-down back is less valuable in full PPR when projected to trail.

### Wide receivers

Prioritize, in order:

1. Route participation and full-time alignment.
2. Target share and targets per route run.
3. Air-yard share / downfield role.
4. Red-zone and end-zone targets.
5. Quarterback quality and expected pass volume.
6. Matchup against the relevant coverage/secondary role.

Important indicators:

- Routes run versus team dropbacks, not just raw snaps.
- Whether the player is a starter in two-WR and three-WR sets.
- Slot versus perimeter alignment when matchup data supports it.
- Target share when teammates were healthy versus absent.
- Target depth and whether production depended on one long play.

Warnings:

- A part-time WR who scored on a long touchdown is usually a low-priority claim.
- Do not overreact to targets gained only in a late blowout.
- A player may lose short-term value when a target-dominant teammate returns.

### Tight ends

Prioritize, in order:

1. Route participation and whether the TE runs routes rather than blocks.
2. Targets per route, especially in the red zone.
3. Full-game snap rate and third-down/two-minute role.
4. Team pass volume and quarterback tendencies.
5. Matchup against linebackers/safeties and opponent TE usage allowed.

Tight end is often a streaming position. A player with a stable route role and a favorable matchup can be more useful next week than a better-known TE with weak deployment.

### Quarterbacks

Prioritize, in order:

1. Rushing floor and designed/goal-line rushing.
2. Dropback volume and pass attempts expected from game script.
3. Opponent pass defense and pressure rate.
4. Healthy receiving corps and offensive line.
5. Implied total, pace, and projected competitiveness.

Streaming rule: favor a quarterback with rushing or a high-volume game environment over a pocket passer who needs exceptional passing efficiency to matter.

### D/ST

Prioritize, in order:

1. Opposing quarterback turnover/sack vulnerability.
2. Opponent implied total and offensive injuries.
3. Defensive pressure rate, sack production, and takeaway ability.
4. Home field, weather, and likelihood of playing with a lead.
5. Avoidance of elite offenses even if the D/ST scored recently.

Do not chase a prior defensive touchdown; those events are highly volatile.

### Kickers

Prioritize, in order:

1. Strong implied team total.
2. Secure job and offense capable of sustained drives.
3. Favorable weather or dome conditions.
4. Game expected to stay competitive.
5. Opponent defense that bends but does not always break.

Kickers are generally one-week streams; do not use significant FAAB on them in standard formats.

## News and Timing Workflow

### Immediately after games (Sunday/Monday)

- Flag injuries, ejections, benchings, role changes, unusual snap distributions, and late-game replacements.
- Compare the player’s usage with prior weeks.
- Separate a genuine workload increase from a game-script outlier.
- Create an initial watch list before consensus waiver articles are widely read.

### Before waiver claims (Monday/Tuesday)

- Confirm injuries and likely depth-chart replacements.
- Rank players by role certainty, then upside, then next-week matchup.
- Consider the manager’s roster: urgent starter need, bench stash, playoff upside, or defensive/kicker stream.
- Set conditional claims when the platform permits: preferred target first, lower-cost alternatives afterward.

### Practice reports (Wednesday–Friday)

- Recheck DNP/limited/full practice designations.
- Update recommendations if a starter returns, a candidate is limited, or a coach clarifies roles.
- Avoid overconfidence before final practice/inactive reports when the role hinges on an injury.

### Game day

- Confirm inactives, weather, emergency quarterback situations, and credible late news.
- If a player becomes inactive, pivot to the next confirmed option; do not cling to an outdated recommendation.

## Scoring Framework

Use a 100-point score to create a consistent initial ranking. Adjust weights by format and roster need.

| Category | Points | What earns points |
|---|---:|---|
| Projected opportunity | 0–30 | Starting role, snap share, routes, touches, target/carry share |
| Role change and durability | 0–20 | Clear injury replacement, promotion, multi-week path, little competition |
| Next-matchup environment | 0–15 | Opponent, total, spread, pace, expected script, weather |
| Scoring/ceiling role | 0–10 | Red-zone work, goal-line role, deep targets, QB rushing |
| Talent and efficiency | 0–10 | Strong per-route/per-touch indicators, explosive or reliable skill set |
| Health and availability | 0–10 | Confirmed active, full practice, low snap-limit risk |
| Roster fit and cost | 0–5 | Fills a need without sacrificing disproportionate FAAB or a valuable player |

### Suggested interpretation

- **80–100:** Priority add. Spend aggressively if the role is credible and the player solves a meaningful need.
- **65–79:** Strong add. Pursue as a starter, injury replacement, or valuable bench piece.
- **50–64:** Conditional add. Good streamer or depth option; cost should remain controlled.
- **35–49:** Speculative stash only. Add when bench space permits or upside is unusually high.
- **Below 35:** Avoid unless league depth makes nearly every starter rosterable.

Subtract points for:

- Unconfirmed injury-dependent role: −5 to −15.
- Clear committee without premium receiving/goal-line work: −5 to −15.
- Production driven primarily by one touchdown or long play: −5 to −10.
- A teammate likely to return and erase the role: −5 to −15.
- Bad offensive environment, quarterback uncertainty, or severe weather: −3 to −10.

## FAAB and Waiver-Priority Rules

### FAAB

Use percentage ranges, not false precision. Adapt for league size, roster depth, remaining budget, and urgency.

| Candidate type | Typical FAAB range | Conditions |
|---|---:|---|
| Clear new every-week RB/WR starter | 20–40%+ | Use the high end only for a strong offense, clear workload, and multi-week path |
| Strong multi-week starter with some uncertainty | 10–25% | Role is useful but could become a committee or shrink on a teammate’s return |
| One-week injury/bye fill-in | 3–10% | Matchup-driven or temporary role |
| High-upside bench stash | 1–8% | Clear talent/path but no immediate start requirement |
| QB/TE/DST/K streamer | 0–5% | Usually avoid substantial spending unless format makes the position scarce |

Rules:

- Spend to win a rare, clear starting role; do not bid merely to appear competitive.
- Avoid large bids on ambiguous committees or touchdown-driven breakouts.
- Preserve meaningful budget for the middle of the season, when injuries and role changes can create higher-value opportunities.
- In very shallow leagues, favor short-term lineup points; in deep leagues, bid more for contingency value and future upside.

### Rolling waivers / priority

- Use a high priority for a player who projects as a durable weekly starter, not merely the week’s most-discussed pickup.
- If no such player exists, consider preserving priority and using free agency after claims clear for streamers and speculative depth.
- Do not burn premium priority on a kicker, ordinary D/ST stream, or replaceable one-week bench player.

## Drop Rules

Before adding a player, examine the drop candidate’s forward-looking role.

Prefer dropping:

- Low-upside bench players with declining snaps/routes/touches.
- Handcuffs with no immediate injury path and little standalone role.
- Players whose opportunity disappeared after a teammate returned or a depth-chart change.
- Redundant bench players at a position of strength.
- Replaceable kicker or D/ST streamers when roster rules permit.

Avoid dropping:

- A proven starter because of one bad matchup or one low-scoring week.
- A player whose underlying usage remains healthy despite poor recent points.
- A high-upside handcuff immediately before a fragile or injured starter’s game.
- A player with a plausible post-bye, post-injury, or role-change payoff unless the waiver target is materially better.

## Recommended Output Format

For each waiver run, produce the following:

### Assumptions

- Scoring format and league size.
- Roster need being addressed.
- Data/news cutoff time.
- Any important unknowns.

### Ranked candidates

Use a table:

| Rank | Player | Position | Add type | Next-week outlook | Why now | Main risk | Suggested FAAB / priority |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Player name | RB/WR/TE/QB/DST/K | Starter / stream / stash | Floor-ceiling summary | Usage + news + matchup evidence | Injury, committee, or matchup concern | Range and confidence |

### Brief reasoning for each player

For each candidate, include:

- **Role:** Expected snaps/routes/touches and whether they are rising, stable, or fragile.
- **News:** Injury/depth-chart information and confidence level.
- **Matchup:** Only the matchup details that materially change the projection.
- **Format fit:** Why PPR, half-PPR, standard, superflex, TE premium, or league depth changes the recommendation.
- **Decision:** Add, conditional add, stash, stream, or avoid.

### Contingencies

List explicit "if/then" pivots:

- If the incumbent starter is ruled out, upgrade the backup from speculative to priority add.
- If the incumbent practices fully, downgrade the backup to stash/avoid.
- If the player is limited or carries a snap-count risk, lower floor and FAAB recommendation.
- If weather becomes severe or the quarterback is inactive, downgrade passing-game options.

---

## Quality-Control Checklist

Before finalizing a recommendation, verify:

- [ ] The recommendation uses current player availability and current injury news.
- [ ] The player’s role is supported by snaps, routes, touches, or a defensible depth-chart change.
- [ ] A one-game box score did not outweigh opportunity data.
- [ ] The next opponent and likely game script were considered after role evaluation.
- [ ] The recommendation identifies whether it is for one week, multiple weeks, or a stash.
- [ ] The downside is named plainly: injury uncertainty, committee risk, difficult matchup, poor offense, or fragile role.
- [ ] FAAB/waiver cost matches role certainty and league context.
- [ ] The suggested drop does not sacrifice more future value than the addition provides.
- [ ] Any time-sensitive assumptions are labeled and can be updated after practice reports/inactives.

## Example Decision Logic

**Example:** A backup RB becomes available after the starter leaves Sunday’s game with an injury.

- Upgrade sharply if the backup handled early-down carries, receiving-down snaps, and late-game work after the injury; the starter is ruled out or cannot practice; and the team is favored next week.
- Keep the bid moderate if the backup played only early downs, another back handled passing downs, and the starter is merely limited rather than ruled out.
- Avoid a major bid if a third back split work evenly, the offense has a very low implied total, or the starter is expected to return.

The principle: the best waiver pickup is not necessarily the player who scored most last week. It is the player whose **future opportunity is clearest and most valuable relative to acquisition cost**.

## Data Source Guidance

Use multiple current sources, prioritizing:

1. Official NFL/team injury reports, practice reports, transactions, and game-day inactive lists.
2. Reliable beat reporters and direct coach/team statements for role context.
3. Play-by-play, snap counts, route participation, target/carry shares, and red-zone usage.
4. Sportsbook lines and projected game environments as one input—not as certainty.
5. Consensus rankings and waiver articles as a cross-check, not a substitute for the evidence above.

When sources conflict, favor the most direct, most recent, and most verifiable report. Clearly state the uncertainty instead of presenting a fragile assumption as fact.
